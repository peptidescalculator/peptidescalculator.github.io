Peptide reconstitution calculator formulas

Reconstitution means adding a solvent to a freeze-dried (lyophilized) peptide so that it becomes a solution. Once you know how much peptide is in the vial, everything else is division. These are the five formulas the calculator uses.

Peptide concentration

concentration (mg/mL) = peptide (mg) ÷ solvent (mL)

This is the formula behind most searches for a peptide concentration calculator. 5 mg in 2 mL gives 2.5 mg/mL.

Solvent volume for a target concentration

solvent (mL) = peptide (mg) ÷ concentration (mg/mL)

Use this when you have a concentration in mind and need to know how much solvent to add. A lower target needs more solvent.

Volume that holds a given amount

volume (mL) = amount needed (mg) ÷ concentration (mg/mL)

This is the mg to mL conversion. Milligrams and millilitres only convert through a concentration, so you need the first formula before this one.

Molarity

molarity (mol/L) = (peptide (mg) ÷ molecular weight (g/mol)) ÷ solvent (mL)

Dividing milligrams by g/mol gives millimoles, and dividing millimoles by millilitres gives mol/L.

Molecular weight from a sequence

molecular weight = sum of residue masses + 18.0153

The 18.0153 is one water molecule for the free ends of the chain.

Peptide molarity calculator

Some protocols give a concentration in molar units instead of mg/mL. To convert, you need the molecular weight. Take it from the certificate of analysis, or work it out from the sequence as above.

Example: 5 mg of a 340.38 g/mol peptide dissolved in 2 mL. First, 5 ÷ 340.38 = 14.69 µmol. Then 14.69 µmol ÷ 2 mL = 7.345 mM, which is 7,344.681 µM.

Bacteriostatic water and other solvents

Bacteriostatic water is sterile water with a preservative, typically benzyl alcohol, added so that a vial can be opened more than once. It is a common solvent for reconstitution, but it is not the only one. The right choice depends on the compound and on your protocol, so check the supplier's documentation.

The calculator works with any solvent volume you type in. It does not recommend a solvent, and it does not account for the volume the powder itself takes up. It assumes the final volume equals the solvent you add, which is the usual convention.

Common peptide calculator mistakes

  • Mixing mg and mcg. 1 mg is 1,000 mcg. Check the unit beside every number.
  • Reading µL as mL. A result of 400 µL is 0.4 mL.
  • Using the wrong molecular weight. A salt form, a metal complex or a modified peptide has a different mass from the bare sequence.
  • Rounding early. Keep the full numbers until the last step.
  • Trusting the sum over the document. If a result disagrees with your protocol or certificate of analysis, the documents win. Look for a unit slip.

A quick sanity check

A vial of 5 mg in 2 mL should come out between 1 and 10 mg/mL. If you see 0.0025 or 2,500 with the wrong unit, a factor of 1,000 has slipped in somewhere.

Peptide calculator FAQ

What is a peptide calculator?

A peptide calculator is a tool that does the arithmetic for working with a freeze-dried peptide in the lab. You enter the amount of peptide in the vial and the volume of solvent, and it returns the concentration. Other modes work backwards from a target concentration, convert to molarity, or find a peptide's molecular weight from its sequence.

How do you calculate peptide concentration?

Divide the amount of peptide by the volume of solvent. For example, 5 mg of peptide in 2 mL of solvent gives 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5 mg/mL. Multiply by 1,000 to express it as 2,500 mcg/mL. Keep the units consistent: milligrams with millilitres, or micrograms with microlitres.

How much solvent do I add to reach a target concentration?

Divide the amount of peptide by the concentration you want. 5 mg at a target of 2.5 mg/mL needs 5 ÷ 2.5 = 2 mL of solvent. A lower target concentration needs more solvent for the same vial, and a higher one needs less.

How do you convert mg to mL for a peptide?

Milligrams measure mass and millilitres measure volume, so they only convert through a concentration. Volume (mL) equals the amount you need (mg) divided by the concentration (mg/mL). For 1 mg from a 2.5 mg/mL solution, that is 1 ÷ 2.5 = 0.4 mL, or 400 µL.

What is the difference between mg and mcg?

1 mg equals 1,000 mcg (micrograms, also written µg). Mixing the two up produces a result that is wrong by a factor of 1,000, which is the most common mistake with peptide arithmetic. Check the unit on every number before you rely on a result.

How do you calculate a peptide's molecular weight?

Add the average mass of each amino acid residue in the sequence, then add one water molecule (18.015 g/mol) for the free N- and C-termini. For GHK that is 57.052 + 137.141 + 128.174 + 18.015 = 340.38 g/mol. The residue masses on this page are the average values published by Expasy.

How do you calculate the molarity of a peptide solution?

Divide the mass in milligrams by the molecular weight in g/mol to get millimoles, then divide by the volume in millilitres to get mol/L. For 5 mg of a 340.38 g/mol peptide in 2 mL, that is (5 ÷ 340.38) ÷ 2 = 0.007345 mol/L, or 7.345 mM.

What is bacteriostatic water?

Bacteriostatic water is sterile water that contains a preservative, typically benzyl alcohol, to stop bacteria growing in a vial that is opened more than once. Whether it suits a given peptide depends on the compound and your protocol, so check the supplier's documentation. The calculator works with any solvent volume you enter.

Does a peptide calculator account for the volume of the powder?

This calculator does not. It assumes the final volume equals the solvent volume you add, which is the usual convention and a close approximation for small amounts of peptide. If your protocol requires more precision, follow its instructions instead.

How long does a reconstituted peptide last?

That depends on the compound, the solvent, the temperature and how the vial is handled, and no single figure is safe to quote for all peptides. Use the stability information in the supplier's documentation or your protocol. This page does not give storage durations.

Is this a peptide dosage calculator?

No. It does lab arithmetic on numbers you enter: concentration, solvent volume, the volume that holds a given amount, molarity and molecular weight. It does not suggest amounts, schedules or syringe settings, and it is not for use in people or animals. Products mentioned are sold for laboratory research only.

Where does the molecular weight data come from?

The residue masses are the average masses published by Expasy, the bioinformatics portal of the SIB Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, and the one-letter codes follow the IUPAC-IUB recommendations. Modified peptides such as acetylated, amidated, cyclic or disulfide-bonded ones differ, so use the figure on your certificate of analysis for those.

Peptide calculator glossary

Lyophilized peptide
A peptide that has been freeze-dried into a stable powder for shipping and storage. It must be dissolved in a solvent before use. Also called a freeze-dried peptide.
Reconstitution
Adding a solvent to a lyophilized powder so that it becomes a solution of known concentration.
Diluent or solvent
The liquid added to a lyophilized peptide. Bacteriostatic water is one common choice; the right one depends on the compound and the protocol.
Concentration
The amount of substance per unit of volume, usually written in mg/mL or mcg/mL for peptides.
Molarity
Concentration expressed as moles of substance per litre of solution, written M, mM or µM. It needs the molecular weight.
Molecular weight
The mass of one mole of a molecule, in g/mol. For a peptide it is the sum of its residue masses plus one water molecule.
Amino acid residue
An amino acid after it has been joined into a peptide chain, which has lost one water molecule. Residue masses are what you add up to get a peptide's molecular weight.
Certificate of analysis (COA)
A supplier document reporting test results for a specific batch, such as purity by HPLC and identity by mass spectrometry. It is the most reliable source for a peptide's molecular weight and content.

Sources and how the numbers are checked

  • Amino acid residue masses: Expasy, SIB Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, average masses for the 20 standard residues.
  • One-letter and three-letter codes: IUPAC-IUB, Nomenclature and Symbolism for Amino Acids and Peptides (Pure Appl. Chem., 1984, 56, 595–624).
